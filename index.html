About the Project

Project Overview

Our project explores crime patterns across Chicago using the City of Chicago's Crimes - 2001 to Present dataset, which contains reported crime incidents from 2001 onward, excluding the most recent seven days.

We focus on the years 2010–2026 to examine how crime profiles differ across Chicago's 77 community areas and how these patterns change over time. We also retain data from 2006 onward to support our earliest five-year trend comparisons.

Our visualization focuses on three main questions:

  • Which crime types are most prominent in Chicago and its communities?
  • How do arrest outcomes differ across crime types?
  • Which crime types are increasing or decreasing in share over time?

Dataset and Variables

Our data comes from the City of Chicago Data Portal. We use the following variables:

  • Primary Type: Category of crime, such as theft or burglary.
  • Year: The year the incident occurred.
  • Arrest: Whether an arrest was recorded for the incident.
  • Community Area: The community area number associated with the incident.
  • Incident Count: Number of records grouped by year, community area, crime type, and arrest status.

We chose community areas instead of wards because they provide a more consistent geographic basis for comparing neighborhood crime profiles.

Visualization Design

Crime Types — Horizontal Bar Chart

Our first visualization uses a horizontal bar chart to compare the frequency of different crime types. We considered using a pie chart, but the large number of crime categories would have resulted in many small slices and crowded labels.

Crime types are represented by vertical position, while bar length represents the number of incidents using a square-root scale. Percentage labels show each crime type's share of reported incidents. The categories are sorted in descending order, and orange highlights the most common crime type. The horizontal layout also accommodates longer crime names.

Arrest Outcomes — 100% Stacked Bar Chart

Our second visualization compares arrest and non-arrest outcomes for each crime type. We chose a 100% stacked bar chart because crime counts vary substantially between categories. Using percentages allows users to compare arrest rates without differences in total incidents overwhelming the visualization.

Each bar represents 100% of incidents for a crime type. Yellow represents arrests and blue represents non-arrests, while segment lengths and percentage labels communicate their respective proportions.

Crime-Share Trends — Diverging Bar Chart

Our third visualization shows how crime-type shares have changed over a five-year comparison window. A central zero line separates increases from decreases, while bar direction, length, and color communicate the direction and magnitude of change.

Changes are measured in percentage points rather than raw incident counts because we are interested in the relative prominence of crime types. Changes within ±1 percentage point are classified as stable.

We considered a multiline chart, but displaying a separate line for every crime type would have made the visualization too cluttered.

Interactive Chicago Map

The map allows users to explore crime patterns geographically across Chicago's 77 community areas. Its sequential color scale uses darker shades to represent higher values of the selected metric. A dropdown allows users to switch between measures without requiring multiple maps.

Interactive Features

  • Year Slider: Filters the charts by the selected year and adjusts the five-year trend comparison window. We chose a slider over buttons because it provides a compact and intuitive way to explore consecutive years.
  • Community Area Selection: Clicking a community area on the map filters all three charts to that location. A reset button restores Chicago-wide results without changing the selected year.
  • Map Zoom and Pan: Allows users to inspect smaller community areas. Zoom-in, zoom-out, and reset-view controls provide additional navigation options.
  • Tooltips: Hovering over a community displays its incident count, most common crime type and share, and arrest share. Trend tooltips display comparison years, underlying counts, starting and ending shares, and percentage-point changes.
  • Map Metric Selector: Allows users to change the metric represented by the map's color scale. The legend updates to reflect the selected measure.

Development Process

Our team divided development into three sequential phases, with each team member responsible for one phase. We spent approximately 20 person-hours developing the application.

Part 1 — Foundation and Initial Charts (7 hours)

The first phase established the HTML structure, JavaScript organization, and data processing pipeline. We implemented the initial crime-type bar chart, 100% stacked arrest chart, and year slider. We organized the code into functions for initialization, updating axes, updating visualizations, and handling interactions.

The stacked bar chart was particularly challenging because understanding and implementing D3's stacking functionality required additional research and debugging.

Part 2 — Chicago Map and Trend Chart (7 hours)

The second phase introduced the diverging trend chart and interactive Chicago map. We developed the calculations for crime shares and percentage-point changes, using course Labs 4–5 as references for the diverging bar chart. We also connected community selection on the map to filtering across the three charts.

Part 3 — Map Color Encoding and Interactions (6 hours)

The final phase focused on implementing the map's metric-dependent color encoding and interactive controls. One major challenge was organizing updates so changes to the selected metric or year were reflected correctly. We also refined the selected-region colors, borders, and tooltips to improve usability and clarity.

Use of AI Tools

We used GitHub Copilot, Claude, and ChatGPT as development support tools. ChatGPT helped with brainstorming visualization designs and interaction techniques, while GitHub Copilot and Claude assisted with debugging, improving visualization clarity, and checking calculations.
